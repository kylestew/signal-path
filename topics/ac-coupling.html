AC Coupling

Block DC, pass changing signals — a basic tool in audio circuits

What It Does

AC coupling removes a steady voltage offset and lets the changing part of the signal continue on. In audio terms: it removes unwanted DC offset while keeping the audio signal.

Why It Matters

  • Keeps one stage's DC offset from being forced into the next stage
  • Helps protect downstream inputs from unwanted DC voltage
  • Common in audio paths where you want the sound, but not the offset

Important: AC coupling is usually for audio, not control voltage. If you AC-couple pitch CV, envelopes, gates, or other slow signals, you will distort or remove the part you care about.

The Circuit

The simplest AC-coupling circuit is a capacitor in series with the signal and a resistor to ground at the output. Together they make a high-pass filter: very slow changes are reduced, faster changes pass more easily. In many real circuits, that resistor is just the input resistance of the next stage. In this example, the source has a DC offset so you can see the capacitor remove it.

Cutoff Frequency

The cutoff frequency tells you where the filter starts noticeably reducing the signal:

fc = 1 / (2πRC)

At the cutoff point, the output is about 70.7% of the input. Below that, the signal keeps dropping at 20dB per decade. Well above it, the signal is only lightly affected.

Rules of Thumb

  • 1μF + 10kΩ → fc ≈ 16 Hz — good general-purpose audio coupling
  • 100nF + 10kΩ → fc ≈ 160 Hz — passes mid and high frequencies
  • 10μF + 1kΩ → fc ≈ 16 Hz — same cutoff, lower impedance path

For eurorack audio signals, aim for a cutoff well below 20 Hz so you do not lose bass or add unwanted tilt to the waveform. 1μF into a typical 100kΩ input impedance gives fc ≈ 1.6 Hz, which is comfortably below the audio band.
