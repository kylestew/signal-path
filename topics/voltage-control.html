Voltage Control / CV

Use voltage to control other parts of a modular synth

What It Is

In a modular synth, control voltage usually called CV is just a voltage used as a message. Instead of carrying sound to your ears, it tells some other part of the system what to do.

A changing voltage can raise pitch, open a filter, move a VCA, change an LFO speed, or sweep almost any parameter that has a CV input.

Why It Matters

  • It is how modules talk to each other
  • It lets one signal shape another signal
  • It turns a fixed patch into something animated and playable

Audio vs CV

Audio and CV are not two different substances. Both are just voltage changing over time. The main difference is how fast they change and what you use them for.

  • Slow voltage → usually used as control
  • Fast voltage → usually heard as audio

This is why an LFO can become audio if you turn it up fast enough, and why an oscillator can be used as a modulation source at audio rate.

Common Kinds Of CV

  • Pitch CV: tells an oscillator what note to play. In Eurorack this is usually 1V/oct.
  • Gate: a voltage that stays high while a note is on.
  • Trigger: a short pulse used to start an event.
  • Envelope: a shaped control signal that rises and falls over time.
  • LFO: a repeating slow control signal for wobble, movement, and rhythm.
  • Random CV: changing voltage used for variation and unpredictability.

Common Voltage Ranges

Different modules use different voltage ranges, so do not assume every CV input wants the same thing.

  • 0V to +5V is common for envelopes, gates, and many modulation signals
  • -5V to +5V is common for bipolar LFOs and centered modulation
  • 1V/oct pitch CV uses voltage differences to represent note spacing

Always check the module documentation if voltage range matters. Some inputs clip gently, some ignore excess voltage, and some behave differently once you go past the expected range.

Unipolar vs Bipolar

A unipolar control signal stays on one side of 0V, for example 0V to +5V. A bipolar signal swings above and below 0V, for example -5V to +5V.

This matters because some destinations expect a positive-only control signal, while others make more sense with movement around a center point.

Attenuation And Offset

Two of the most useful CV tools are attenuation and offset.

  • Attenuation: makes the CV smaller
  • Offset: shifts the whole CV up or down

Example: if an LFO is swinging from -5V to +5V but your destination wants 0V to +5V, you can reduce the depth and add positive offset to move it into the useful range.

DC Coupling

CV paths usually need to be DC-coupled. That means steady voltages are allowed to pass through. If you AC-couple a CV path, slow control signals can droop, distort, or disappear.

That is why audio outputs are often AC-coupled, while CV inputs and outputs usually are not.

Rules Of Thumb

  • CV is just voltage used as a message
  • Pitch, gates, envelopes, and LFOs are all forms of CV
  • Audio can be used as fast CV, and slow CV can become audio if it speeds up enough
  • Check whether a signal is unipolar or bipolar before patching it somewhere important
  • Use attenuation and offset to make modulation fit the destination
  • Use DC coupling for CV paths unless you specifically want to remove slow or steady voltage
